# Evaluation: AWS m7i.large on-demand price in us-east-1 (January 2028 forecast) and whether to buy a 3-year commitment now

**Decision objective:** Decide whether to buy a 3-year AWS commitment now (Standard or Convertible Reserved Instance, EC2 Instance Savings Plan, or Compute Savings Plan) for m7i.large-class capacity in us-east-1, or stay on on-demand or a shorter term. Nobody can know the January 2028 on-demand price; it is a future fact. The decision should instead rest on things we can find out: today's on-demand and commitment rates, AWS's historical record of on-demand price changes for previous-generation instances, how likely and how fast newer generations replace m7i, and how sure the requester is about their own usage over 36 months. The output should be a range of scenarios and a break-even analysis, not a single point forecast.

## Verdict

The January 2028 on-demand price of m7i.large in us-east-1 cannot be determined. AWS does not publish future prices and no forecast was found (E15). It is also the wrong basis for the decision: a 3-year commitment's rate is fixed at purchase, so its value depends on usage across all 36 months (E15). The evidence supports a conditional decision rule instead. The packet gives indicative 3-year No Upfront rates of $0.0511/hr for a Compute Savings Plan and $0.0457/hr for an EC2 Instance Savings Plan, against $0.1008/hr on-demand (E1, E3). At full use these save about 49% and 55%. They break even at about 51% and 45% average utilization of the committed amount, or after about 18.3 and 16.3 months of full use if usage then stops (K1-K4, K10, K11). The packet found no on-demand cuts to general-purpose x86 instances since about 2016 (E7). That points to a near-zero base rate, but it is inferred from absence within a limited search, so a price cut alone is unlikely to wipe out the discount. Price-cut risk and utilization risk do compound, though. A 5% cut moves the Compute SP break-even only from about 51% to 53% utilization. A 45% GPU-style cut, which has no general-purpose precedent, would push it to about 92% (Compute SP) or 82% (EC2 Instance SP) (K30-K32, E5). The biggest risk is utilization, and the requester has not specified it (E16). Recommendation, conditional on missing inputs. (1) Make a 3-year commitment only for the usage floor you are confident will run at full use for at least 24 months. At today's prices that is about 6 months beyond the Compute SP's 18.25-month full-use break-even (K10, K11). After a 5% on-demand cut that break-even rises to about 19.2 months, which still leaves about 5 months of margin (K43). Leave the rest on on-demand or a shorter term. (2) The plan-type choice is tentative. It rests on a 0.54-cent/hr gap between two rates taken from one INFERRED aggregator snippet (E3). On those figures, if the workload is near-certain to stay on m7i, the m7i EC2 Instance SP is cheaper: the Compute SP commits about 11.8% more, about $142 per instance-equivalent over the term (K23, K42). The Compute SP's main value is insurance. If a move off m7i is forced, an m7i-locked plan is stranded for its whole remaining commitment, up to about $1,201 per instance-equivalent for the full term (K9, E11), while a Compute SP keeps applying to the new family. A voluntary migration saves money under a Compute SP only if the fleet can cut instance-hours in line with the performance gain, or if other usage absorbs the surplus coverage. For a single instance or a fixed-size fleet it does not save. One m8i.large under a $0.0511/hr Compute SP would cost about $0.056/hr, which is more than the $0.0457/hr m7i EC2 Instance SP (K36). A $0.0511/hr commitment covers about 1.24 m7g.large, so moving one instance to m7g saves nothing (K37). For fleets that can shrink in proportion, the Compute SP beats the m7i lock only if the move to M8i comes within roughly the first 6 months (K26). That window runs from about 0 to about 11 months if the EC2 Instance SP discount is 1 point higher or lower (K38, K39). The equivalent Graviton window, about 16 months, also assumes performance parity (K29). All of these windows also assume successor families get the same percentage discount, which the packet does not confirm. (3) Do not delay for months to choose between m7i and M8i. At packet rates, delaying more than about 3.7 months costs more in forgone savings than the whole 36-month penalty of committing on m7i rather than M8i (K40, K41). A short delay is worth it only to verify rates. Verify every rate against the AWS Price List API before buying. No Savings Plan rates for flex or Graviton instances were available.

## Criteria

### C1 Effective discount and break-even of 3-year commitment options versus current on-demand (weight 25)

At the indicative 3-year No Upfront rates, the Compute Savings Plan costs 50.7% of on-demand (a 49.3% discount) and the EC2 Instance Savings Plan costs 45.3% (a 54.7% discount) (K1-K4). The gap between them is about 5.4 points of the on-demand rate (K5). Measured against the EC2 Instance SP's own rate, the Compute SP commits about 11.8% more spend for the same m7i usage (K23). Over 26,280 hours the committed spend is about $1,342.91 (Compute SP) and $1,201.00 (EC2 Instance SP), against $2,649.02 for always-on on-demand (K7-K9). Break-even utilization of the commitment is about 50.7% for the Compute SP and 45.3% for the EC2 Instance SP. If the workload runs at full use and then stops, the commitment breaks even after about 18.25 months (Compute SP) or 16.3 months (EC2 Instance SP) (K10, K11). The packet has no us-east-1 rates for 1-year terms, Standard RIs, Convertible RIs, Partial Upfront or All Upfront, so those options cannot be compared exactly. [E1, E3, E4, E8, E10, E16]

*Mechanisms:* A Savings Plan is a fixed $/hr spend charged for every hour of the term, whether or not it is used. Savings therefore equal (utilization x on-demand rate - committed rate) x hours, and the break-even utilization is the committed rate divided by the on-demand rate. On-demand is billed only when used, so it costs nothing when idle. A Compute SP applies its commitment across instance families (plan C3), so any eligible usage in the account or organization can absorb commitment that the m7i workload leaves unused. In a mixed estate its effective under-use risk is therefore lower than that of an EC2 Instance SP locked to one family. The packet does not verify which services count as eligible (for example Fargate or Lambda).

*Strengths:*
- The $0.1008/hr on-demand baseline comes from two aggregators (E1, CORROBORATED). It also matches m7i.8xlarge at $1.6128/hr divided by 16 (K17, E10), although that aggregator data may share an upstream source with E1. The m6i x 1.05 match (K15, E8) is circular, because E8's 5% ratio was itself derived from $0.1008, so it is not independent support.
- A discount of about 50% leaves a wide margin against modest price cuts or modest under-use (K20, K32).

*Weaknesses:*
- The commitment rates come from a single third-party snippet (E3, INFERRED), and earlier search summaries were inconsistent.
- There are no us-east-1 figures for RIs or for upfront payment options (E3 limitations); E4 is multi-region only.
- The break-even analysis assumes Linux, shared tenancy and no licensing, none of which the requester has confirmed (E16).

### C2 Historical behaviour of on-demand prices for prior EC2 generations (m4/m5/m6i) after newer generations launched (weight 15)

The packet points to a near-zero recent base rate for on-demand cuts to general-purpose x86 instances. The most recent M-family cuts found were 5% to C4/M4 in January 2016 and further M4/T2 cuts in December 2016 (E7); no later general-purpose cut was found, which is an inference from absence. m7i.large's current list price is 5% above m6i.large's current list price (K15, E8). E8 is a single current-price snippet with no price history, so it does not show that m6i was never cut. AWS still cuts on-demand prices on existing instance types, but the recent example was supply-driven GPU instances: P5 up to 45%, P4d up to 33% (E5). Capacity Block prices for H200 went up about 15% (E6). Scenarios for the m7i.large on-demand price over 2026-2029: the base case is unchanged at about $0.1008/hr; a mild case is a cut of about 5% (2016-style); an extreme case is a 45% cut (GPU-style, very unlikely for general-purpose instances). Upward movement is possible, but no evidence shows it for general-purpose on-demand. Price cuts and under-use interact. A 5% cut from day one raises the Compute SP break-even utilization from 50.7% to about 53.4% (K32), and its full-use-then-stop break-even from about 18.25 to about 19.2 months (K43). A 45% cut raises the break-even utilization to about 92% (Compute SP) or 82% (EC2 Instance SP) (K30, K31). So the extreme scenario would make any commitment that is not almost fully used a loss. [E5, E6, E7, E8, E15, E3, E1]

*Mechanisms:* Since about 2016, AWS appears to pass on hardware efficiency gains mainly by launching new generations at similar or slightly higher instance prices with better performance, rather than by repricing older types. Large cuts have happened where supply and demand shifted sharply, as with GPUs. When AWS does cut prices, Savings Plans already bought keep their old rates and only new purchases get the lower rates (E5). A cut therefore lowers the on-demand price the commitment is compared against, while the committed cost stays the same, and that raises the break-even utilization.

*Strengths:*
- The 2025 GPU cuts come from an official AWS announcement (E5, CORROBORATED), and the 2016 M4 cuts from corroborated reporting (E7).

*Weaknesses:*
- The 'no cuts since 2016' finding rests on absence of evidence within a 12-search budget (E7 limitations; G2 unresolved).
- No systematic price history for m5, m6i or m7i was retrieved. E8 gives only a current m6i price, not its history.
- The m6i price comes from a single aggregator snippet (E8).

### C3 Generation-obsolescence risk (m8i/Graviton successors) and commitment flexibility (weight 20)

A successor is already available. M8i and M8i-flex have been GA in us-east-1 since August 28, 2025, with AWS claiming up to 15% better price-performance and up to 20% more performance than M7i (E9). Per instance-hour, M8i costs about 5% more than M7i (K16, E10). With a 20% performance gain, that is about 12.5% less per unit of performance (K18). What an m7i lock costs depends on whether leaving m7i is optional or forced, and on whether the fleet can shrink. (a) Fixed-size fleet or a single instance, where the move is voluntary. A Savings Plan charges its committed $/hr whatever runs, so moving to a faster family saves nothing unless fewer instance-hours are then needed. One m8i.large under a $0.0511/hr Compute SP would cost about $0.056/hr, because the commitment covers only about 95% of the higher M8i SP rate and the rest is billed at on-demand. That is more than the $0.0457/hr m7i EC2 Instance SP (K36). A $0.0511/hr commitment covers about 1.24 m7g.large, so moving a single instance to m7g saves nothing (K37). In this case the m7i EC2 Instance SP is cheaper unless the move is forced. (b) A fleet that can cut instance-hours in proportion to the performance gain, or that has other usage to absorb surplus coverage. Assuming M8i gets the same percentage Compute SP discount, a Compute SP moved to M8i costs about $0.0447 per m7i-equivalent unit of performance (K24). That is only about 2.2% below the m7i EC2 Instance SP (K25). Because the Compute SP costs about 11.8% more before the move (K23), it comes out ahead only if the move happens within roughly the first 6 months (point estimate about 5.6, K26). The window is very sensitive to the rates: with the EC2 Instance SP discount 1 point of on-demand lower or higher, it runs from about 0 to about 11 months (K38, K39). Moving first and then buying an M8i EC2 Instance SP would cost about $0.0400 per m7i-equivalent unit (K27). For m7g, at performance parity, which the packet does not establish, the equivalent window is about 16 months (K28, K29). (c) A forced move off m7i, for example because of a feature or capacity need, an architecture change, or a Graviton port that is actually carried out. An m7i-locked EC2 Instance SP or Standard RI is then stranded. The loss is the whole remaining commitment, up to about $1,201 per instance-equivalent over the full term (K9), with no general exit (E11). It is not just the 2.2% forgone gain. A Compute SP would keep applying to the new family. M8i-flex is described as having 5% lower prices than M8i (E9), but the packet has no rate for it or for m7i-flex. For reference, M8i on-demand costs about $0.088 per m7i-equivalent unit (K22). Conclusion: the EC2 Instance SP is the cheaper choice only if staying on m7i is near-certain. The Compute SP premium, about $142 per instance-equivalent over the term (K42), is best seen as insurance against a forced move, against changes of region or OS, and as a way to let other usage absorb the commitment. Both conclusions depend on the single-source rates in E3. [E9, E10, E13, E3, E1, E8, E11, E16]

*Mechanisms:* An instance-family-locked discount applies only to m7i usage. If the workload leaves m7i, the locked commitment is still charged but covers nothing. A Compute SP applies its fixed $/hr commitment across families, but it is still a fixed $/hr charge. A faster successor lowers cost only when the requester can then run fewer instance-hours, or can put the surplus coverage to other use. Fleet size and divisibility therefore decide whether the migration benefit exists at all. A Convertible RI can be exchanged for other families (E13), but the packet has no us-east-1 rate for it.

*Strengths:*
- Successor availability and the performance claims come from AWS's own announcement (E9, CORROBORATED).
- The m7i-to-m8i 5% per-instance premium (K16) is consistent with the 15% price-performance and 20% performance claims (E9).

*Weaknesses:*
- The performance figures are vendor 'up to' claims (E9). Gains on a specific workload may be smaller, which would shrink the migration benefit further.
- The Compute SP and EC2 Instance SP rates for m8i, m8i-flex, m7i-flex, m7g and m8g are not in the packet. K24-K29 and K36-K39 assume each family gets the same percentage discount as m7i.
- K24-K29 assume instance-hours can be cut in proportion to the performance gain; the requester's fleet size is unknown (E16).
- The payback windows are highly sensitive to the 0.54-cent/hr gap between two rates from one INFERRED snippet (E3; K38, K39).
- The m8i.large and m8g.large prices were not confirmed, and the m8i comparison uses 8xlarge figures from different aggregators (E10).
- Graviton price-performance relative to m7i was not researched (G3 unresolved), and the m7g price is a single snippet (E10 limitations).
- The Convertible RI exchange rule (equal or greater value) was not verified (E13).

### C4 Certainty of the requester's own m7i.large usage over 36 months (weight 25)

This is the most important input, and it is missing. The request gives no usage level, confidence, OS, tenancy or cost of capital (E16). The break-even analysis shows the commitment pays only if average utilization of the committed $/hr stays above about 51% (Compute SP) or 45% (EC2 Instance SP) over 36 months (K1, K2). At 70% average utilization the Compute SP saves about $511 per instance-equivalent over the term (K20); at 50% it loses about $18 (K21). In an estate with other eligible compute, the Compute SP's utilization is measured across that wider usage, so its effective break-even risk is lower than these single-workload figures suggest (C1 mechanisms). The recommendation can therefore only be conditional. Make a 3-year commitment only for the floor of usage you are confident will run at full use for at least 24 months, and leave the rest on on-demand. That clears both break-evens at today's prices with about 6 months of margin (K10, K11). After a 5% on-demand cut the Compute SP break-even rises to about 19.2 months, leaving about 5 months (K43). A deeper cut would erode the margin further (K30, K31). [E16, E3, E1, E11]

*Mechanisms:* The commitment cost is sunk and noncancellable (E11). Each idle committed hour is a pure loss, while each used hour saves about half the on-demand rate, so losses from under-use build up as fast as savings from use. A later on-demand price cut would raise the utilization needed to break even (K30-K32) and lengthen the full-use months needed (K43).

*Strengths:*
- The break-even thresholds follow directly from the rates and need no forecast (K1, K2, K10, K11).

*Weaknesses:*
- The requester's usage and confidence cannot be researched (G4 unresolved, E16).
- The thresholds inherit the uncertainty in the single-source rates (E3).
- Whether the requester has other compute that could absorb unused Compute SP commitment is unknown (E16).

### C5 Payment option economics (No/Partial/All Upfront) and cost of capital (weight 10)

This cannot be evaluated for us-east-1. The packet has no Partial or All Upfront m7i.large rates for the region (E3 limitations), and the requester's cost of capital is not given (E16). The only upfront data are multi-region monthly averages for 3-year All Upfront (E4). There the average Convertible RI costs about 21% more than the average Standard RI (K19), which suggests flexibility costs noticeably more under RIs. That figure is not region-specific. [E4, E3, E16, E11]

*Mechanisms:* Paying upfront buys an extra discount in exchange for giving AWS the cash early. It is worth it only if the extra discount exceeds the return the requester could earn on that cash over the term, i.e. the present value of the avoided monthly payments discounted at the cost of capital. Upfront payment also increases the amount lost if usage falls, because payments are nonrefundable (E11).

*Strengths:*
- E4 shows the direction of the Standard versus Convertible gap.

*Weaknesses:*
- The E4 figures are multi-region, and the currency unit is assumed.
- There are no us-east-1 upfront rates and no discount rate, so a present-value comparison cannot be done.

### C6 Exit and resale options (RI Marketplace eligibility, Savings Plan non-cancellability) (weight 5)

Exit options are narrow. Savings Plans and RIs are noncancellable, payments are nonrefundable apart from narrow exceptions, and Savings Plans cannot be resold (E11). The packet does not say what the narrow exceptions are. For example, it cannot confirm whether a short post-purchase return window exists for small Savings Plans, and no further research was possible. Only Standard RIs can be sold on the RI Marketplace, and since January 15, 2024, RIs bought at a discount (for example through private pricing) are restricted from resale (E12). Convertible RIs cannot be resold but can be exchanged (E13). Since June 1, 2025, commitments are limited to a single end customer, so partners cannot share them across customers (E12). [E11, E12, E13]

*Mechanisms:* With no general exit, the worst case for a commitment is the full remaining commitment. The only partial exits are Marketplace resale (Standard RIs only, probably at a discount to make the sale), exchange (Convertible RIs, within the same commitment), and whatever narrow exceptions apply under E11, which the packet does not identify.

*Strengths:*
- Each rule comes from a CORROBORATED secondary source: noncancellability and no Savings Plan resale from E11, and Marketplace resale restrictions from E12. The two sources cover different points, so they do not corroborate each other.

*Weaknesses:*
- Primary AWS documentation could not be fetched (G5 only partially resolved).
- The 'narrow exceptions' to nonrefundability are not identified (E11).
- Which 'discounted' RIs are excluded from resale still needs confirmation (E12).

## Alternatives

- **Stay fully on-demand:** No lock-in and no utilization risk, but at full use it costs about twice the committed rate: $2,649 versus $1,343 (Compute SP) per always-on instance over 3 years (K7, K8). Best if usage is unlikely to last beyond about 18 months, or will be below about 51% of any committed amount (K1, K10).
- **3-year Compute Savings Plan, No Upfront:** About a 49.3% discount (K3), but it commits about 11.8% more than the EC2 Instance SP for the same m7i usage, about $142 per instance-equivalent over the term (K23, K42). It applies across instance families, so it keeps covering usage after a forced move off m7i instead of being stranded (E9, E11). A voluntary move saves money under it only if instance-hours can be cut in proportion to the performance gain. For a fixed-size fleet, one m8i.large under it costs about $0.056/hr (K36). Under divisible-fleet and same-discount assumptions, it beats the m7i lock only for a move to M8i within roughly the first 6 months (range about 0-11 months, K26, K38, K39), or to Graviton within about 16 months at performance parity (K29). It also suits plausible region or OS changes, or other eligible compute that can absorb unused commitment. It cannot be cancelled or resold (E11).
- **3-year EC2 Instance Savings Plan, No Upfront, on m7i:** About a 54.7% discount (K4), but locked to the m7i family. On packet figures it is the cheaper choice only if staying on m7i is near-certain. If a move off m7i is forced, the remaining commitment is stranded, up to about $1,201 per instance-equivalent for the full term (K9, E11).
- **Move or test first, then buy an EC2 Instance Savings Plan on the target family (M8i or Graviton):** If the workload will move, committing on the target family avoids both the Compute SP premium and stranding. An M8i EC2 Instance SP would cost about $0.0400 per m7i-equivalent unit of performance, assuming the same discount and a fleet that shrinks in proportion (K27), against $0.0457 for the m7i lock. The packet does not confirm the M8i or Graviton Savings Plan rates. Testing should take weeks, not months, because each month of delay costs about $40 per instance (K35, K41).
- **Graviton (m7g/m8g):** m7g.large lists at $0.0816/hr, about 19% below m7i.large per instance (K33, E10). It needs an ARM port, and its performance relative to m7i is not established. The m8g price was not confirmed (G3 unresolved). If the port is feasible and benchmarked, committing on Graviton after the move may be cheaper than committing on m7i. The packet lacks Graviton Savings Plan rates to confirm this.
- **Flex variants (m7i-flex, m8i-flex):** M8i-flex is described as offering 5% better price-performance at 5% lower prices (E9). The packet has no us-east-1 rates for M8i-flex or m7i-flex, so these cannot be quantified. They are worth pricing for workloads that do not need sustained full CPU.
- **Spot for interruptible parts of the workload:** The per-AZ range that is probably Spot, $0.0383-$0.0445/hr, is at most about 44% of on-demand (K34, E2). Spot can be interrupted and the label is ambiguous, so it suits only fault-tolerant parts of the workload. Whatever moves to Spot should be left out of the commitment.
- **3-year Standard RI:** No us-east-1 rate was found. Multi-region data suggest it is the cheapest form of commitment (E4). It is the only option that can be resold, with restrictions (E12), but it is locked to the instance attributes.
- **3-year Convertible RI:** No us-east-1 rate was found. Multi-region averages put it about 21% above a Standard RI (K19, E4). It can be exchanged to M8i (E13) but cannot be resold.
- **Delay, or buy in staggered tranches, or use a 1-year term:** No 1-year us-east-1 rates were found. Waiting costs about $40 per always-on instance per month in lost EC2 Instance SP savings (K35). At packet rates, more than about 3.7 months of delay costs more than the whole-term penalty of committing on m7i rather than M8i (K40, K41), so a long delay to choose the family is not justified. A short delay to confirm official Price List rates is reasonable. Buying in tranches spreads exposure to rate errors and usage uncertainty, but each tranche held back costs the same monthly amount. The tranche approach does not rely on any return window, since the packet does not identify one (E11).

## Tradeoffs

- Discount versus flexibility: the EC2 Instance SP's lower rate (the Compute SP commits about 11.8% more, about $142 per instance-equivalent over the term, K23, K42) buys an m7i lock-in. The Compute SP's flexibility pays mainly as insurance against a forced move off m7i, which would otherwise strand up to about $1,201 per instance-equivalent (K9, E11). It also pays for region or OS changes and in a mixed estate that can absorb unused commitment. A voluntary migration gains under it only if instance-hours can be cut (K26, K29, K36).
- Coverage depth versus utilization risk: committing above the confident floor increases savings at high use but loses money below about 51% (Compute SP) or 45% (EC2 Instance SP) utilization (K1, K2, K21). A price cut would raise these thresholds (K30-K32).
- Committing now versus deciding the family first: waiting costs about $40 per instance-month (K35). At packet rates, more than about 3.7 months of waiting costs more than the whole-term wrong-family penalty (K40, K41), so the family decision should be quick.
- Upfront payment versus liquidity: upfront options may add discount but tie up nonrefundable cash (E11). They cannot be quantified here (E16).
- Resale versus flexibility: a Standard RI can be resold with restrictions (E12), while a Convertible RI can be exchanged but not resold (E13).

## Edge cases and failure modes

- A forced move off m7i while an EC2 Instance SP or Standard RI is locked to m7i strands the whole remaining commitment, up to about $1,201 per instance-equivalent over the full term (K9, E11). The move might come from a feature or capacity need, an architecture change, or a Graviton port that is actually carried out (E9). This is far larger than the roughly 2.2% forgone gain (K25), which applies only when the workload can stay on m7i.
- A small or fixed-size fleet moves to a faster family under a Compute SP. The fixed $/hr commitment is still charged in full. One m8i.large would cost about $0.056/hr, because part of the hour is billed at on-demand (K36), and one m7g.large uses only part of a $0.0511/hr commitment (K37). The migration saving does not appear.
- AWS cuts the m7i on-demand price, or the rates for new Savings Plan purchases, after you buy. Existing commitments keep the old rate (E5), and break-even utilization and months rise (K30-K32, K43).
- The commitment rates in E3 turn out to be wrong, since they come from a single snippet. Every break-even and migration figure would shift; the payback windows are especially sensitive (K38, K39). Recompute them from the Price List API.
- M8i or Graviton Savings Plan discounts differ from m7i's. K24-K29 and K36-K40 assume the same percentage discount.
- The OS is not Linux, or the tenancy is dedicated. Licensing changes both on-demand and commitment rates (E16).
- The workload shrinks or ends before about 16-18 months, so the commitment loses money (K10, K11). Savings Plans offer no exit apart from narrow exceptions that the packet does not identify (E11).
- An RI bought through private pricing or another discount cannot be resold (E12).
- Use across multiple accounts or end customers: since June 2025, commitments cannot be shared across end customers (E12).
- Aggregator labelling errors, such as Spot prices labelled as on-demand (E2), contaminate any analysis built on them.

## Counterarguments

- **AWS might cut on-demand prices by 2028, making a commitment bought today poor value.** — No general-purpose x86 on-demand cut since about 2016 was found (E7), which suggests a near-zero base rate, though this is an inference from absence. A 5% cut only moves the Compute SP break-even from about 51% to 53% utilization (K32), or from about 18.25 to 19.2 months of full use (K43). A 45% GPU-style cut would push break-even to about 92% (Compute SP) or 82% (EC2 Instance SP) (K30, K31). Such a cut is unlikely, but it would turn any under-used commitment into a loss. The risk therefore falls mostly on commitments sized above the confident usage floor, which supports sizing conservatively.
- **The EC2 Instance SP's larger discount makes it the better buy.** — On packet figures this holds only if staying on m7i is near-certain. In that case the Compute SP's extra 11.8% (K23) buys little, because a voluntary move recovers at most about 2.2% per unit of performance, and only if instance-hours can be cut (K25). For a fixed fleet a voluntary move recovers nothing (K36, K37). If a forced move off m7i is a real possibility, the EC2 Instance SP risks stranding up to about $1,201 per instance-equivalent (K9, E11), against a Compute SP premium of about $142 (K42). The answer turns on how likely a forced move is, and on a rate gap from one INFERRED snippet (E3).
- **Prices could rise, so locking in now is protective.** — The only increase found was for H200 Capacity Blocks, and AWS reportedly said it would not raise On-Demand or Savings Plan prices (E6). That is weak support for buying commitments as an inflation hedge.
- **Without the requester's usage data, no recommendation is possible.** — Partly true (E16). The break-even thresholds (K1, K2, K10, K11) still give a usable rule: commit only to the usage floor you are confident will run at full use for at least 24 months. That leaves about 6 months of margin at today's prices and about 5 months after a 5% cut (K43).

## Practical implications

- Before any purchase, pull the us-east-1 Linux shared-tenancy SKUs from the AWS Price List API for m7i.large and for the candidate families (m8i, m8i-flex, m7i-flex, m7g, m8g): on-demand, Savings Plan and RI rates, for all terms and payment options. Then recompute K1-K14 and K23-K43.
- Make a 3-year commitment only for the usage floor you are confident will run at full use for at least 24 months. That leaves about 6 months of margin above the 18.25-month Compute SP break-even at today's prices, and about 5 months after a 5% cut (K10, K11, K43). Cover the rest with on-demand, Spot (for interruptible parts) or shorter commitments.
- Treat the plan-type choice as tentative until rates are verified (E3; K38, K39). If staying on m7i is near-certain, the EC2 Instance SP is cheaper on packet figures (K23). If a forced move off m7i is a real possibility, a Compute SP's premium (about $142 per instance-equivalent, K42) insures against stranding up to about $1,201 (K9). Do not count on migration savings under a Compute SP unless the fleet can cut instance-hours after moving (K36, K37).
- Treat Graviton as a candidate worth benchmarking. m7g.large is about 19% cheaper per instance (K33), subject to ARM porting, performance testing and confirmed Graviton Savings Plan rates.
- Do not wait for a 2028 price signal: no general-purpose cut is likely, and existing commitments would not benefit from one anyway (E5). Keep any family-decision delay short. Each month costs about $40 per always-on instance (K35), and more than about 3.7 months costs more than the whole-term wrong-family penalty (K40, K41). Buying in tranches spreads rate and usage risk, but it does not rely on any return window (E11).
- Provide usage history, confidence, fleet size and whether it can shrink after a migration, the likelihood of a forced move off m7i, OS, tenancy, cost of capital, and whether other compute could absorb a Compute SP, so the conditional recommendation can be made specific.

No us-east-1 rates were found for RIs, 1-year terms or upfront payment options.
- Savings Plan rates for m8i, m8i-flex, m7i-flex, m7g and m8g are not in the packet, and no new research was possible. K24-K29 and K36-K41 assume the same percentage discount as m7i, and on-demand prices for m7i-flex, m8i-flex and m8g are missing.

## Further research that could change the conclusion

- Official AWS Price List data for m7i.large in us-east-1 across all commitment types, terms and payment options. A much smaller discount would raise the break-even utilization, and a different Compute SP vs EC2 Instance SP gap could reverse the plan-type preference (K38, K39).

- Workload-specific benchmarks of M8i and Graviton against m7i, and the cost of an ARM port.
- The requester's 12-36 month usage forecast and its variance, whether other compute could absorb a Compute SP, and their cost of capital.
- A systematic on-demand price history for m5, m6i and m7i. A documented general-purpose cut would raise the price-cut risk.
