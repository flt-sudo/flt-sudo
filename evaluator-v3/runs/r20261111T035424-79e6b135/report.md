# Evaluation: Year-3 Brazil Market Share Forecast and Entry Decision (Fleet Telematics, 2027 Entry)

## 1. Verdict

**There is no year-3 market share number that the evidence can support, and 15% is not one of the plausible ones.** The CEO's figure comes from a single statistic: 9 of 14 people at our own trade-show booth said they would "consider" switching to us, with no price mentioned. That statistic cannot carry a forecast. The materials also leave open the market's size (it varies 5x depending on definition), what we would charge, who we compete against and at what shares, how long ANATEL certification takes (it decides when selling can start), and whether we have a local partner.

**What I recommend telling the board:**
1. **The share figure.** Do not present 15%. If the board insists on a number, present an illustrative range of roughly **0.5–3% of commercial fleets by year 3**, with the central case around 1%, and label it as an assumption-driven scenario rather than a forecast (derivation in section 5). Even that range rests on assumptions the strategy team has not checked.
2. **The entry decision.** Hold off on full entry for now; this is "not yet", not "no". Do not approve the $2.4M year-1 budget as a commitment. Instead, approve a funded discovery phase of about 90–120 days, roughly $150–300k (my estimate). It has explicit kill/go criteria (section 9) and would end in a go/no-go decision around Q2 2027. Brazil may well be attractive, but nothing in these materials shows it yet.

## 3. Detailed analysis

### 3.1 Why "64% would consider" does not become "15% share"

Seven separate problems stand between the survey result and any share figure:

1. **Sample size.** 9/14 = 64%, but the 95% Wilson confidence interval is about **39% to 84%**. Even on its own terms, the point estimate is little better than a coin flip with a lean.
2. **Selection bias.** Respondents were recruited *at our booth*. Fleet managers who walk up to a vendor's booth are already curious about that vendor, or at least about switching. The result describes our booth visitors, not Brazilian fleet managers. The bias inflates the figure and cannot be corrected after the fact.
3. **Question wording.** "Would you consider switching" asks about willingness to evaluate, not intent to buy, which is the weakest rung of the funnel. Even consumer purchase-intent models such as ACNielsen BASES assume only part of the *top box* ("definitely will buy") converts, for example 75% of top-box respondents. "Would consider" sits well below top box. The intent literature (Morwitz; Chandon, Morwitz & Reinartz 2005; Sun & Morwitz 2010) documents systematic overstatement, especially when answering costs nothing.
4. **No price.** Price was never mentioned. In a market where one incumbent is rumoured to give hardware away, interest measured without a price tells us little about purchases at a price.
5. **Interviewer and setting effects.** Answers given face-to-face to the vendor's own staff at its own stand lean towards politeness. Respondents are unlikely to say "no" to our faces.
6. **Switching friction in B2B telematics.** Hardware is installed in vehicles, contracts run for years, drivers are trained, integrations exist, and in Brazil telematics is often tied to theft recovery and insurance requirements. Each of these lowers conversion from "consider" to "switch".
7. **The 64% to 15% step is not explained.** No funnel, reach assumption or time path links the two numbers. 15% is about a quarter of 64%, which looks like an intuitive discount rather than a calculation. To reach 15% of the market within about 2–3 selling years, we would need to reach most of the market, get it to evaluate us, and win roughly 1 in 4 of all fleets that evaluate. That is far beyond typical new-entrant win rates against entrenched incumbents.

### 3.2 The denominator problem

- **Fleets vs vehicles.** The notes count *fleets* (180k–900k). Telematics markets are normally measured in *active units/vehicles* (Berg Insight counts active systems). A 15% share of fleets and a 15% share of vehicles are very different targets. Small fleets are numerous and cheap to win; large fleets hold most of the vehicles and revenue.
- **All fleets vs telematics adopters.** Share of all commercial fleets is a different metric from share of fleets already using telematics. Third-party estimates put Brazilian fleet-management penetration somewhere around 20–40%, depending on the segment and the source (Ken Research cites ~23.5% for 2025; Frost & Sullivan cited ~40% for connected trucks in 2021). Those estimates are low-to-moderate confidence and not directly comparable.
- **Market value estimates disagree by about 10x.** Third-party estimates for Brazil range from about $224M (MarketsandMarkets, "automotive telematics") to about $2.4B (Mobility Foresights, "vehicle telematics"). That spread is itself a measure of how loosely the scope is defined.

Until the board agrees which denominator it means, "15%" has no fixed meaning.

### 3.3 Competitive context (external benchmarks)

- Berg Insight (via reseller listing) puts active fleet-management systems in Latin America at about **8.3M in 2024**, forecast to reach about 16.6M by 2029 (15% CAGR). It names Gurtam, Michelin Connected Fleet (which owns Brazil's Sascar) and Geotab as regional leaders.
- **Geotab**, the world's largest telematics provider by many measures, reported **200k Latin American subscriptions in January 2023** and **over 300k in 2025**. Against about 8.3M regional active systems, that is roughly **3–4% share across the region**, achieved after years of investment and an extensive reseller network.
- Frost & Sullivan describes Brazil's connected-truck telematics market as "moderately fragmented", with **about 55% held by the top 5 providers** (Sascar, Positron, Omnilink, Autotrac named). Several of these are entrenched local players with theft-recovery and insurance tie-ins.

**Implication:** 15% in year 3 would mean a new entrant with no partner, a 9-person team and uncertain certification reaching a share several times larger than a global leader's regional share. That is not credible without some exceptional advantage, and none is described in the materials.

## 4. Strengths and weaknesses of the current case

**Strengths**
- There is some interest signal. 9 of 14 booth visitors did not reject us outright, which is enough to justify further research.
- The market is large and growing. The Latin American active base is forecast to roughly double from 2024 to 2029, so a growing market leaves room for new entrants.
- The break-even target (4,000 fleets) is modest relative to even the low TAM estimate (about 2.2% of 180k).
- The team has already identified the key unknowns, such as ANATEL and incumbent bundling. The notes are honest about what they do not know.

**Weaknesses**
- The demand evidence is not decision-grade (section 3.1).
- There is no denominator, no pricing, no competitor shares and no partner.
- The break-even figure assumes US pricing in a market where at least one competitor reportedly gives hardware free. Local pricing is likely lower, which raises the break-even fleet count.
- The year-1 budget includes certification, but certification may take up to 18 months. Year 1 could therefore be spent largely unable to sell hardware.
- The share target and the break-even target are not reconciled, so the plan cannot be checked for internal consistency.

## 5. Quantitative analysis

### 5.1 What 15% would mean in absolute terms

| Denominator | 15% share = fleets | vs break-even (4,000) |
|---|---|---|
| 180k fleets (narrow) | 27,000 | 6.75x break-even |
| 900k fleets (broad) | 135,000 | 34x break-even |
| Telematics-adopting fleets only (~23.5% of 180k–900k ≈ 42k–212k) | 6,300–31,700 | 1.6–7.9x |

Winning 27k–135k fleets in about 2–3 selling years, from zero, with a 9-person team, is not plausible.

### 5.2 Break-even as a share

- Of all fleets: 4,000 / 900k = **0.44%** to 4,000 / 180k = **2.2%**.
- Of telematics-adopting fleets: about **1.9% to 9.4%**.
- **Price sensitivity.** If Brazilian pricing turns out about 40% below US pricing (an illustrative assumption given free-hardware competition) and costs stay largely fixed, break-even rises to roughly 4,000 / 0.6 ≈ **6,700 fleets**. Under narrow definitions, *break-even alone* could then require a meaningful share.

The useful board question is therefore not "will we reach 15%?". It is: **can we reach about 0.5–2% (4,000–7,000 fleets) at Brazilian prices before the money runs out?**

### 5.3 Illustrative funnel to year 3 (scenario, not forecast)

Share ≈ reach × consideration in the general population × evaluation rate × win rate.

| Parameter | Low | Central | High | Rationale |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Market reached by sales/marketing by yr 3 | 15% | 30% | 50% | 9-person team, no partner; partner-led channel lifts this |
| "Would consider" (general population, not booth) | 20% | 32% | 45% | Booth 64% deflated for selection bias; CI floor 39% |
| Consider → formal evaluation | 20% | 30% | 40% | B2B switching friction, contracts |
| Evaluation → win | 15% | 25% | 35% | New entrant vs incumbents with bundling |
| **Resulting share** | **0.09%** | **0.72%** | **3.2%** | |

The central case comes to about 0.7%, which is near break-even under the broad TAM and short of it under the narrow TAM. The high case needs everything to go well at once, a local partner included. These parameters are judgment calls; the table's purpose is to make the assumptions explicit so they can be tested, not to claim precision.

### 5.4 Timeline effect

If certification takes 18 months from early 2027, hardware sales may not begin until mid-to-late 2028. "Year 3" (2029) would then be only about 1–1.5 years of selling, which pushes all scenarios lower unless we use already-certified partner hardware.

## 6. Comparisons and alternatives

| Option | Cost/risk | When it fits |
|---|---|---|
| **A. Full entry per budget ($2.4M yr 1)** | High; commits before demand, pricing or certification are known | Only after discovery validates the key assumptions |
| **B. Staged discovery, then go/no-go (recommended)** | Low (~$150–300k, my estimate); delays entry about 4–6 months | Now; resolves the main unknowns cheaply |
| **C. Partner-led entry** (reseller/distributor with installers and ANATEL-certified hardware) | Moderate; lower margin, faster reach, shorter certification path | If discovery finds a credible partner; addresses reach and the timeline problem together |
| **D. Software-only / hardware-agnostic entry** (run on incumbents' or third-party certified devices) | Lower capex; sidesteps the free-hardware bundling | If our differentiation is software or analytics |
| **E. Acquire a small local player** | High capital; buys share, customers and certification | If Brazil is strategic and fast share matters more than cost |
| **F. Do not enter / pick another market** | Opportunity cost | If discovery shows price compression makes break-even unreachable |

## 7. Tradeoffs

- **Speed vs evidence.** Waiting 4–6 months costs some first-mover time. But no move into Brazil is "first": incumbents and global players (Geotab, Michelin/Sascar) are already established, so the cost of delay is small compared with the cost of a $2.4M miscommitment.
- **Margin vs reach.** A partner channel cuts margin but may be the only way to reach enough fleets for break-even in a fragmented, installer-dependent market.
- **Precision vs honesty with the board.** Giving the CEO a single number looks decisive. A number without support risks anchoring the board and creating an accountability problem when year-3 results come in far below 15%.

## 8. Edge cases and failure modes

- **Free-hardware incumbent.** If the rumour is true, the price comparison buyers make is "free device + subscription" vs ours, and conversion at US pricing could be near zero in price-sensitive segments.
- **Certification slippage.** At the 18-month end, year 1 burns budget with little revenue.
- **Segment mismatch.** Booth visitors at a São Paulo logistics show likely skew towards mid-to-large logistics fleets. If break-even needs 4,000 fleets, the plan may need many small fleets, a segment the survey barely represents.
- **Theft-recovery orientation.** Much of Brazilian vehicle tracking is driven by security and insurance (rastreamento). If our product is efficiency-focused and lacks recovery services, the value proposition may not match what buyers prioritise.
- **Currency risk.** BRL revenue against USD costs; a depreciation erodes unit economics.
- **Metric ambiguity.** Year 3 arrives, someone reports "share" on a different denominator, and the result can be dressed up or criticised as needed. Fixing the denominator now prevents this.

## 9. Recommended go/no-go criteria for discovery

Fund a 90–120 day discovery with these deliverables:

1. **Market definition.** Agree the denominator (vehicles or fleets; all fleets or adopters; target segments) and size it with a defensible source (e.g., Berg Insight Brazil tables, ANTT/RNTRC registered-carrier data, Frost & Sullivan).
2. **Pricing study.** Get incumbent price points, including verification of the free-hardware rumour, and test willingness to pay *with price shown*, using van Westendorp or conjoint.
3. **Representative demand research.** A sampled survey of n≥150–200 fleet managers drawn from a frame (not a booth), stratified by fleet size and sector, asking purchase intent at a stated price.
4. **Hard demand signals.** At least 5–10 letters of intent or paid pilots from target-segment fleets.
5. **Regulatory.** A written ANATEL certification plan from a local certification lab with a dated timeline, or confirmation that partner hardware is already certified.
6. **Partner.** At least one credible distribution or installation partner shortlisted, with indicative terms.
7. **Rebuilt business case.** Break-even recomputed at Brazilian pricing, with the share needed for break-even stated against the agreed denominator.

**Go** if break-even at local pricing needs no more than about 2% of the agreed denominator within 3 years, pilots or LOIs convert, and certification (or a partner workaround) fits within about 9 months. **No-go or re-scope** otherwise.

## 10. Counterarguments considered

- **"Directional data is better than none; 64% is high."** It is directional, but towards "booth visitors are curious", not towards market share. The confidence interval runs from 39% to 84%, and the sampling bias pushes the true population figure lower by an unknown amount.
- **"Ambitious targets motivate teams."** A target is fine. A *forecast* presented to the board as an expectation is different, and it will be used to judge the investment. Present the target separately from the estimate.
- **"Competitors entered Brazil successfully."** Geotab did, and after years it holds roughly 3–4% of the Latin American active base. That benchmark argues against 15% in 3 years, not for it.
- **"Discovery delays us."** A delay of 4–6 months against a 3-year horizon is small. Spending $2.4M and then finding certification takes 18 months, or that prices are 40% lower, is far worse.

## 11. Practical implications for the board meeting

- Replace "15% by year 3" with this: "We cannot yet forecast share credibly. Break-even needs about 4,000 fleets, or roughly 0.5–2% of the market depending on definition, before adjusting for local pricing. Illustrative year-3 scenarios run from about 0.1% to 3%, central about 1%."
- Ask the board to approve the discovery budget and criteria, not the entry budget.
- Make sure the CEO is not surprised in the meeting. Give him the funnel table beforehand so the change of position comes from him.

## 12. Remaining uncertainty

- Every external market figure here comes from third-party summaries (Ken Research, MarketsandMarkets, Mobility Foresights, Frost & Sullivan, a Berg Insight reseller listing) that I could not check in full text. They disagree widely and I treat them as low-to-moderate confidence.
- The Geotab share comparison mixes subscriptions/vehicles with fleets and covers all of Latin America rather than Brazil alone, so it is order-of-magnitude only.
- The funnel parameters in section 5.3 are my judgment, not empirical Brazilian B2B conversion data. I found no B2B-specific intent-to-purchase conversion benchmark.
- I did not independently verify ANATEL certification timelines; the 6–18 months comes from the team's notes.
- Nothing in the materials describes our product's differentiation. A strong advantage, such as a materially lower total cost or unique video-safety AI, would shift the scenarios upward.
