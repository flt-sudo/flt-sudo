# Evaluation: Whether a Sev-2 hotfix (PAY-4471, PR #8812) can be deployed during the holiday change freeze on the on-call lead's approval alone, without CAB

**Decision objective:** Decide which approval path is actually required to deploy the PAY-4471 hotfix tonight: on-call lead sign-off only per the runbook, a written freeze exception from the CAB chair and VP Engineering, Emergency CAB, deferral until the freeze ends, or the normal non-freeze change process if the freeze is not in effect yet. Also decide whether the Sev-2 classification and the stated impact justify an exception at all, given that a manual workaround exists.

## Verdict

Under the evidence supplied, the on-call lead's sign-off alone is not a valid approval path for PR #8812 during the freeze. Change Management Policy v4 took effect on 2026-09-01 and states that it supersedes any team runbook that conflicts with it [E1]. That overrides the 2023 runbook's rule letting Sev-1 and Sev-2 fixes skip CAB [E2]. Under v4, on-call leads may approve rollbacks only, not forward fixes [E4]. During an active freeze, a Sev-2 forward fix is deferred unless the CAB chair AND the VP Engineering grant a written freeze exception [E3]. Emergency CAB is only for Sev-1 [E5]. The ticket shows no outage, no data loss and no security incident, and a workaround exists, so Sev-1 is not supported [E6, E7]. One premise also looks wrong. Today is 2026-11-11, which is 34 days before the freeze start the user stated (Dec 15) [E8, K2]. If 'tonight' really means 2026-11-11, the freeze rules (6.1 to 6.3) do not apply yet, and the normal non-freeze change process governs. That process was not supplied, so its exact approvers cannot be determined. Section 6.4 sits directly after the freeze provisions in the supplied excerpt, so the evidence does not establish whether it also applies outside a freeze [E4, E5, E8]. Recommended action: (a) if the freeze has not started, send PR #8812 through the normal change process now, before Dec 15, which avoids needing an exception at all; (b) if the freeze is actually active, either defer to Jan 5 or ask the CAB chair and VP Engineering for a written exception. During the freeze, deploying on the on-call lead's approval alone breaches v4 [E3, E4]. Outside the freeze, not relying on on-call-only approval is our recommended precaution, not a rule the supplied evidence establishes. Whether the impact justifies an exception depends on conditions the evidence cannot settle. Against an exception: a manual workaround exists, at about 6 cases a day, or about 132 cases over the 22-day freeze [E7, K3, K4], and the retry carries an unverified double-refund risk, because no idempotency, rollback-plan or test-coverage evidence was supplied [E10, E11]. For an exception: if finance is thinly staffed over the holidays (its actual staffing is unknown), generic industry guidance on accounts payable and wires, not specific to refunds, suggests manual high-value payment handling becomes more error- and fraud-prone [E19, E20, E21, E22]. Refunds may also face external rules on timing: the 14-day EU consumer-withdrawal limit [E13], and a Visa five-day credit window that appears only in 2016 to 2021 Visa guidance and third-party summaries, is unverified against current Visa rules, and has a disputed clock start [E14, E28, E29, E30]. 'Credit not processed' chargebacks are a further exposure [E16, E17]. These external rules also govern how a refund is made, not only when. EU consumer withdrawal refunds must use the same means of payment unless the consumer expressly agrees and pays no fees [E24], and card-scheme guidance expects a credit to the original card [E25, E26]. The manual workaround is therefore adequate only if two conditions hold: (1) finance keeps pace through the freeze, and (2) the back-office tool refunds by the same means or to the original card. The tool's payment rail, the refund type and the original payment method are all unknown [E22, E31]. If condition (2) fails for consumer or card refunds, the workaround has a compliance and chargeback problem of its own, however quickly finance works [E31]. The exception case is moderate if both conditions hold. It is stronger if a holiday backlog is likely or the workaround pays out on a different rail, though fixing the workaround's routing is another remedy for the second problem. In every case the exception remains a discretionary decision by the CAB chair and VP Engineering, weighed against the unverified double-refund risk [E3, E10, E11]. None of this changes the approval path [E3, E4].

### C5 Business impact and adequacy of the workaround (weight 10)

The impact is real. Whether it justifies an exception depends on whether the manual workaround holds through the freeze, and that turns on two conditions: timing and method. Finance handles about 6 failed refunds a day manually [E7]. Over a 22-day freeze (Dec 15 to Jan 5 inclusive) that is about 132 manual cases [K3, K4]. Those cases involve at least about EUR 1.32M of refunds, because each one exceeds EUR 10k [K7]. That is value processed manually, not money lost. The stated figures imply about 2,000 refunds over EUR 10k a day, or at least about EUR 20M a day in high-value refunds [E9, K1, K6]. That is plausible only for a very large payments platform, and refund metrics were not supplied [E9]. Against an exception: the workaround exists and is operating, so deferral is viable [E7]. For an exception, there are four points. (1) Staffing, conditionally. Finance's actual holiday staffing is unknown [E22]. If it is thin, generic industry guidance says holiday cover brings 'vacation coverage gaps', less careful approvals and elevated fraud timing [E19, E21], and manual payment handling is more prone to errors and fraud than automation [E20]. Keeping segregation of duties for about 6 such refunds a day could then be hard [E21]. This guidance is about accounts payable and wires, not refunds, and comes from syndicated media and bank marketing content, so it is weak support. As my own inference, not a source statement: an unrecovered duplicate or misdirected payment would cost up to the full refund amount (over EUR 10k), while most other errors, such as delays, wrong references or misdirections that are recovered, would cost much less [E20]. (2) Timing rules may apply. If these are EU consumer withdrawals, the trader must refund within 14 days [E13]. If they are Visa card refunds, older Visa guidance and third-party summaries refer to a five-day credit window [E14, E28, E29]. Late credits can draw chargebacks [E15], and unprocessed credits expose the merchant to 'credit not processed' disputes (Visa 13.6, Mastercard 4853), with fees and dispute workload on top of the refund [E16, E17]. PSD2 D+1 execution matters only if the company is itself the payer's payment service provider [E18]. (3) Method rules may apply, whatever the timing. CRD Art. 13(1) requires consumer withdrawal refunds to use the same means of payment unless the consumer expressly agrees and pays no fees [E24]. Card-scheme guidance expects a credit to the original card, with an alternate form of credit only as a fallback [E25, E26]. If the back-office tool pays out on a different rail (for example a bank transfer for a card purchase), the workaround may be non-compliant however quickly finance works. It would also leave the card purchase without a matched credit, so 13.6/4853 exposure stays open and a double payout becomes possible [E31]. The tool's rail is unknown [E22]. (4) Customers waiting on delayed high-value refunds carry reputational cost, though no complaint data was supplied [E22]. Contradictions in this area: (a) The earlier synthesis E23 said these rules govern 'when, not how'. E31 supersedes it on method, because E31 is later and rests directly on the same-means text (E24) and the scheme original-card guidance (E25, E26) that E23 did not account for. The timing half of E23 still holds [E23, E31]. (b) E26 (several acquirer and PSP summaries: refund to the same card) and E27 (one US PSP: unlinked Mastercard credits are not expressly prohibited) remain partly unresolved. E26 has more sources, and E27 addresses unlinked card credits, not off-network payouts, so for Mastercard the main consequence of a different-means refund may be chargeback exposure rather than a direct rule breach. Neither affects the CRD rule [E26, E27, E24]. (c) E28 (Visa's own documents: clock from the transaction date or the credit issue date) and E29 (one merchant-services guide: clock from agreeing to refund) are unresolved. Visa's own documents carry more authority but are 2016 to 2021 guidance, and the current Visa Core Rules could not be retrieved [E28, E29, E30]. The five-day figure is therefore unverified. The deciding facts are unknown: finance's holiday capacity, the refund type, the original payment method and the back-office rail, contractual SLAs, PSP status and the manual error rate [E22]. The exception case is moderate if finance keeps pace and the tool refunds by the same means or to the original card. It is stronger if a backlog is likely or the rail departs from the same-means requirement, although fixing the workaround's routing is an alternative remedy for the latter [E31]. It remains a discretionary decision for the approvers under 6.3 [E3]. [E7, E9, E13, E14, E15, E16, E17, E18, E19, E20, E21, E22, E23, E24, E25, E26, E27, E28, E29, E30, E31, E3]

*Mechanisms:* A manual workaround turns an availability problem into an operational-cost, human-error, timeliness and payment-method problem. Its timeliness depends on staff capacity, which may fall over the holidays when fraud and approval risk are said to rise. External timing rules run per refund, so they are breached only when the manual queue falls behind. Method rules (same means of payment, credit to the original card) are breached by how the refund is paid, regardless of speed. The longer the deferral, the more cases go through the manual path.

*Strengths:*
- A workaround exists and is operating [E7]
- If the back-office tool credits the original instrument and finance keeps pace, the workaround can meet both timing and method rules [E31]

*Weaknesses:*
- Implied volume unchecked; ambiguity over whether '~6/day' counts failures or workload [E9]
- The staffing argument rests on generic, non-refund-specific media and marketing content, and actual finance staffing is unknown [E19, E20, E21, E22]
- The back-office rail is unknown, so the workaround may breach same-means or original-card requirements even when on time [E22, E24, E25, E31]
- Refund type, finance holiday roster, SLAs and manual error rate are unknown, so it is unknown which rules apply or are at risk [E22]
- The regulatory and scheme evidence rests on search snippets, some dated (Visa 2016 to 2021) or secondary; the current Visa rules were not retrieved, and the Visa clock start is disputed [E13, E14, E15, E16, E17, E18, E24, E25, E26, E27, E28, E29, E30]

### C6 Change risk and rollback readiness (weight 10)

The only evidence that the change is low-risk is 'reviewed, passing CI' [E7]. There is no evidence on idempotency of the retried ledger call, test coverage of the timeout path, or a rollback plan [E11]. Payment-provider guidance is to use idempotency keys when a timeout leaves the outcome of a state-changing call such as a refund uncertain, because a blind retry can create a duplicate [E10]. A retry on refund calls over EUR 10k that are not idempotent could cause double refunds of at least EUR 10k each [E10, E7]. Approvers should require proof of idempotency and a rollback plan before granting any exception or approving the change. Under 6.4, rolling back is the one action the on-call lead could approve if the fix misbehaves [E4]. [E7, E10, E11, E4]

*Mechanisms:* A timeout does not tell the caller whether the server committed the operation. If the retry has no idempotency key, a committed-but-timed-out refund can be applied twice.

*Strengths:*
- The change is small in scope (timeout config plus retry) and passed review and CI [E7]

*Weaknesses:*
- Idempotency, tests and rollback plan unknown [E11]
- The external guidance comes from search snippets only and may not reflect how the internal ledger behaves [E10]

## Tradeoffs

- Speed versus compliance. Deploying tonight on the on-call lead's sign-off during the freeze is fast but breaches v4 6.3 and 6.4 [E3, E4]. Getting two senior approvals or using the normal process takes longer but is compliant.
- Manual-workaround risk versus change risk. Deferral keeps about 6 manual cases a day [E7, K4]. If staffing is thin, error and fraud risk are higher and timing rules may be breached, and a different-rail payout risks breaching method rules [E19, E20, E21, E31]. - Deploying before the freeze avoids the exception requirement, but it still needs the normal change process and the same idempotency checks [E8, E11].

## Edge cases and failure modes

- The on-call lead might hold delegated VP Engineering or CAB chair authority, which would change who can sign [E12].
- A newer policy version or an existing exception could change the answer [E1, E12].
- The freeze dates may differ from what the user stated, or the scenario date may be hypothetical [E8].
- Other on-call staff may still follow the stale runbook and create further non-compliant changes [E2].
